Research brief · critical reading

Market Wizards: Two Questions for US Equity Research

Information responseFundamental fragilityBooks

This brief draws on agent-assisted critical reading of three Market Wizards books by Jack D. Schwager. The work separated interview accounts, author commentary, and agent inference. It generated research questions, not newly discovered or validated alpha; no backtests were run. The main value is in specifying conditions for interpreting evidence and revising decisions.

Conditional Information Response

When does an earnings surprise contain information that prices have not already absorbed? Michael Masters describes a stock rising before an announcement and barely reacting to apparently good news. Michael Marcus describes abandoning a position when favorable news produces an unexpected price response.

These accounts motivate a conditional question: among comparable earnings surprises, does the combination of the pre-announcement price path and initial response help distinguish prior incorporation from delayed adjustment? The comparison should assess what this combination adds beyond earnings surprise, momentum, and the initial response individually. Subsequent outcomes must begin after all classification inputs become observable.

This overlaps with established earnings-drift research. Guidance, simultaneous news, risk repricing, and liquidity pressure are competing explanations. Historical consensus vintages, comparable first-reported earnings, announcement timestamps, prices, and concurrent disclosures would be essential. A price run-up alone cannot establish informed trading.

Fundamental Fragility

When do operating inconsistencies add information beyond accounting-quality measures? Steve Watson connects customer experience and returns with manufacturing orders and reported sales; Dana Galante describes changing market attention to cash flow.

A narrower question is whether contemporaneously available operating evidence helps distinguish subsequent cash conversion among firms with similar accounting measures. This requires dated, comparable evidence within a sector. Customer accounts may be selective, and weak cash conversion can reflect legitimate investment. Incremental forecasting information would not by itself establish mispricing or tradable returns.

Open research question

Both directions remain hypotheses. What observable evidence would best distinguish information already reflected in prices from delayed adjustment, and separate either from changes in fundamentals or liquidity?
